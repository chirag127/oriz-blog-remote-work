Most home-office advice is a shopping list. A standing desk, a second
monitor, a plant. Buy the list and you still can't concentrate at 3pm,
because none of it touches the two problems that actually break focus
at home: there is no boundary between work and everything else, and
there is no signal that the workday has begun.

Fix those two things first. The furniture can wait.

## Start with a boundary, not a desk

The single biggest predictor of whether you can focus at home is
whether you can create a boundary — physical or ritual — around work.
A separate room with a door is the strongest version. If you have one,
use it, and close the door even when you're alone. The closed door is
not for other people. It's a message to yourself.

Most of us don't have a spare room. That's fine — a boundary can be
smaller than a room:

- A specific corner that is *only* for work. Not the sofa, not the bed.
  Your brain learns the association fast.
- A desk that faces a wall or a window, not the rest of the flat. What
  you can see, you will think about.
- A "closing" ritual at the end of the day: laptop shut, notebook
  closed, chair pushed in. This is the door for people without a door.

The goal is a place your body recognises as work, and a way to leave it.

## The startup signal

At an office, the commute is the signal. You travel, you arrive, and
your brain flips a switch. Remote, that switch never flips — so the
morning bleeds into a fog of email and half-attention.

Build your own signal. It should be short, physical, and the same
every day:

- Make coffee and carry it to the desk. The walk is the commute.
- Open the one document you're working on *before* you open email or
  chat. First contact with the day should be the work, not other
  people's requests.
- Write the day's single most important task on paper. One line. If you
  finish nothing else, finish that.

Do the same three things in the same order and within a week your
attention shows up on cue.

## Light, sound, and the two-metre rule

Now the physical layer — and here the advice is short because most of
it doesn't matter as much as people claim.

**Light.** Put the desk near a window if you can, facing it or beside
it. Natural light during the day keeps your sleep steady, which is the
real lever on afternoon focus. If the room is dark, a bright, cool desk
lamp during work hours does most of the same job.

**Sound.** You need a way to signal "do not interrupt" and a way to
control noise. Over-ear headphones do both — they tell the household
you're working and they let you choose what you hear. If you share the
space, agree on a visible sign for "in a meeting."

**The two-metre rule.** Keep your phone at least two metres away during
focus blocks. Not face-down on the desk — across the room. The cost of
standing up is exactly the friction you need. Everything within arm's
reach will be reached for.

## Chair and screen: the only things worth spending on

If you're going to spend money, spend it here, because these two touch
your body for eight hours a day.

A chair that supports your lower back matters more than any desk. You
don't need the famous expensive one — you need one that fits *you*,
which usually means trying it. And raise your screen so the top of it
sits at eye level; a stack of books works as well as a monitor arm. A
neck that isn't tilted down all day is worth more than a second
monitor.

Everything else — the standing desk, the ring light, the mechanical
keyboard — is preference, not focus. Buy it when you want it, not
because a list told you to.

## A room you can leave

The best remote workspace isn't the most equipped. It's the one you can
switch on in the morning and switch off at night. A corner your brain
knows, a signal that starts the day, a chair that doesn't hurt, and a
door — real or ritual — you can close.

Get those right and the shopping list becomes what it should have been
all along: optional.
